A privately prepared Texas mineral ownership report is **a set of sourced claims to examine, not an ownership certificate**. Before relying on its tract description or stated fraction in a buyer-offer discussion, make a report-to-source register: note who prepared it, its issue and as-of dates, the interest it describes, every cited instrument, the scope of the search, and each unresolved qualification. Compare a cited county-record reference with the actual writing when available. Mark a missing source or conflict as unresolved rather than turning the report’s conclusion into a verified number.

This is an educational source-check method, not a title opinion, legal advice, a net-mineral-acre calculation, an appraisal, or a judgment that a buyer’s offer is fair. Keep the actual report, ownership documents, and buyer correspondence private. The [title, lease, and ownership pillar](/learning-center/title-lease-ownership/) explains how the underlying record types fit together; this guide addresses the narrower task of auditing a report you have already received.

## Start with the report’s own scope

Do not assume every document called a “mineral ownership report” follows one format. Record its preparer, client or intended use if stated, issue date, effective or as-of date, counties and tracts covered, records examined, exclusions, and any disclaimer. If the report does not say whether a search included older records, probate material, or later filings, record that silence. Ask the preparer what was reviewed rather than silently treating an online index search as an exhaustive examination.

The [Texas General Land Office’s mineral-rights FAQ](https://www.glo.texas.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Minerals%20FAQ_updated%202023.pdf) says determining ownership of a mineral interest requires examination of the deed and lease records affecting the claim. It also explains that the GLO holds original land-grant material, while later private conveyance records are generally in the deed records of the county where the tract lies. That distinction is why a private report should expose its underlying record trail rather than ask you to accept a cover-page ownership conclusion.

## Make a report-to-source register

Copy a report field exactly as written. Keep its cited source and its qualification next to it. The statuses below describe the **quality of the trail available to you**, not whether the claimed interest is legally correct.

- **Preparer and dates:** copy the name, issue date, as-of date, and any revision. Mark each as stated, missing, or changed; ask what date the conclusion actually covers.
- **Tract:** preserve the county and full legal-description wording. Compare the wording with the cited instrument and mark it located, partial, conflicting, or unresolved.
- **Interest type:** copy “mineral,” “royalty,” “overriding royalty,” or another label exactly as printed. Ask whether the cited writing addresses that type of interest; mark any label conflict.
- **Fraction or acreage basis:** preserve the number and units without converting a payment decimal into ownership. Record whether the report shows its source and assumptions or leaves the method unresolved.
- **Instrument trail:** list the parties, instrument number, book and page, and recording date if supplied. Mark whether the actual writing was obtained, only an index lead was found, or nothing was located.
- **Qualifications:** list every exclusion, exception, adverse claim, and missing link. Mark what remains explicitly open or unclear and what has been referred for qualified review.

An “instrument located” status means you found a document to read. It does **not** mean the document conveys the interest claimed by the report or that no later instrument changes the result. Likewise, an “as-of” date does not certify what happened afterward. The [Texas State Library’s property-research guide](https://www.tsl.texas.gov/ref/propertyresearch) identifies county offices as the custodians of local real-property records and notes that online access and older-record formats vary by county.

## Follow the citation to the writing, not just the search result

If the report cites an instrument number or book and page, use the named county clerk’s record system or contact that office to locate a copy. Preserve the county, source URL or office, retrieval date, and the writing’s own parties and legal-description wording. If you find only an index hit, say “index lead only” in your register. If no citation is supplied, ask the preparer which writings support the tract and fraction instead of guessing from a similarly named owner.

Read the report’s exceptions alongside the documents it cites. A reservation, later conveyance, probate reference, or inconsistent tract description may require case-specific interpretation; this article does not resolve it. The general [Texas deed-and-title guide](/blog/how-texas-mineral-rights-ownership-works-deeds-conveyances-and-title/) explains those mechanics. A qualified Texas title professional or attorney should evaluate an unresolved chain, disputed wording, or competing claim before anyone treats a number as a transferable interest.

Do not use a tax or operating record to fill a title gap. The [Railroad Commission of Texas royalties FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) points readers to county courthouse documents for private lease and royalty agreements and separately describes the Commission’s reported production and permitting records. The RRC says it lacks authority over private lease and royalty matters. A well or production hit may help identify operating context; it does not authenticate the owner or the report’s fraction.

## Keep the buyer letter as a separate next step

Only after the report’s tract, interest label, source trail, and unresolved items are visible should you read a buyer letter against the same identifiers. Record where the letter uses the report’s assumptions, where it differs, and what the buyer says remains subject to title review. Do not silently carry a report figure into an offer worksheet as a confirmed net mineral acre or royalty decimal. The [offer-review pillar](/offer-review/) covers written terms and comparison questions; this report audit does not estimate value or decide whether to sell.

If a source is missing or the documents conflict, keep both versions, ask the preparer for the cited basis, and seek qualified Texas title review for the unresolved ownership question. That is a useful outcome of the register: a precise question for the right professional, rather than a premature ownership conclusion.
