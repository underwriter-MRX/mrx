# North Dakota Mineral Rights Probate: Form 11 vs. Form 12

The [North Dakota Courts informal-administration guide](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf) names two different personal-representative deeds. It labels **Form 11** for distributing real property to heirs or devisees and **Form 12** for a personal representative's sale of real property. The instructions for both point to the recorder in the county where the property is located and describe an accompanying certified copy of the representative's letters. The guide's inventory illustration includes oil or mineral rights as real property. These are descriptions of the court's _source documents_, not a way to decide which deed—or whether either deed—fits a particular inherited interest.

If you are researching a North Dakota mineral interest after a death, first identify what question the estate papers actually leave open: Was a particular interest part of this estate? Was real property distributed to an heir or sold by the personal representative? What county and property description do the papers identify? A royalty statement, well result, or search-index match may help locate records, but it does not answer those legal questions alone. Keep the exact references and ask qualified North Dakota counsel what the documents mean for this estate.

## Why mineral rights appear in a probate-deed search

The court guide's sample inventory lists “oil or mineral rights” among examples of real property. In that same illustration, it distinguishes solely held property, property held with others as tenants in common, and non-probate holdings. Its example is a prompt to **identify the actual ownership and estate papers**, not proof that every family mineral interest belongs to a probate estate or passes through an informal administration. The source is the [North Dakota Courts guide, printed page 4, Figure A](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf).

This matters when an inherited royalty check is the only clue in hand. A check may carry a payor name, owner number, property label, or legal-description fragment. It does not identify the decedent's exact retained interest, settle a reservation in an earlier deed, show the estate's authority, or identify who legally received the property. For the separate operator-contact or payment question, see [North Dakota Inherited Royalty Questions: Records and Ombudsman](/blog/north-dakota-inherited-royalty-questions-records-and-ombudsman/). That article routes agency and payment inquiries; this one stays with the court guide's two deed labels and recording-source role.

## What the guide distinguishes

| Guide label                                             | Purpose the guide describes                                                     | What the label alone cannot decide                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Form 11: Personal Representative's Deed of Distribution | A personal representative's distribution of real property to heirs or devisees. | Whether the reader is an heir or devisee of this particular interest, whether the property was in this probate estate, or whether the form is correct for these facts. |
| Form 12: Personal Representative's Deed                 | A personal representative's sale of real property.                              | Whether this estate authorized or completed a sale, what was sold, what a contract requires, or whether a particular buyer acquired the interest.                      |

The guide makes that distinction in its distribution discussion and in the [Form 11](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf) and [Form 12](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf) instruction sections (PDF pages 41 and 45). The two labels do not mean that an heir gets to choose between a sale and a distribution. They describe different transactions in the guide. The estate's actual authority, documents, property description, and legal path require case-specific review.

An instrument name is therefore a useful **research filter**, not a title conclusion. If a record search surfaces “Deed of Distribution,” that name tells you what to ask the recorder or lawyer to retrieve. If the papers instead refer to a personal representative's sale, Form 12's label helps you frame a different question. Neither search result establishes that the exact mineral interest you are investigating was included, that the signers had the necessary authority, or that a missing instrument should be created now.

## What the county-recording direction does—and does not—say

The [Form 11 instructions](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf) direct a deed of distribution to the county recorder where the real property is located. The [Form 12 instructions](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf) give the same county-location rule for the sale deed. Each instruction section also describes recording with a certified copy of the representative's Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration certified on or after the deed date. That is what the court's self-help source says; it is **not** an instruction here to record a particular instrument or a statement that a record was properly filed for this estate.

For a research inquiry, the county question is practical: which North Dakota county does the actual property description place the interest in, and what recorded references already exist there? The county named on an old royalty statement may be a lead, but a statement's payor label is not a legal description or a recorder index. The [North Dakota Department of Mineral Resources' mineral-owner page](https://www.dmr.nd.gov/dmr/oilgas/mineralownerinfo) directs private-interest record questions toward county recording. Its well information is not an estate or title determination.

If the county is still uncertain, keep the uncertainty visible. Preserve the exact wording, source, date, and property reference from each paper you already have; do not silently change a tract label into a legal description or assume a nearby well fixes the property location. A qualified North Dakota professional can help reconcile conflicting references before anyone relies on a particular county record.

## Build a private source list before asking which path applies

A concise list makes the professional question more answerable without turning a public article into a deed tutorial. Record only what is already available:

1. **The estate reference.** Note the decedent's name as shown on the court or estate papers, any case or appointment reference already provided, and whether a personal representative is identified. Do not infer authority from a family relationship alone.
2. **The property reference.** Preserve the county and legal description as they appear on an actual deed, estate schedule, or other identified source. If the papers use different descriptions, list each source separately rather than merging them.
3. **The instrument reference.** If a recorder search yields a candidate deed or assignment, save its type, parties, date, county, and instrument or book/page identifier. Label it a candidate until its full text and the surrounding chain are reviewed.
4. **The question, not a conclusion.** Write whether you are trying to understand a distribution, a personal-representative sale, or an unexplained absence of a record. Keep a separate question for payor-account changes or unpaid royalties.

This is a research checklist, not a required filing packet. Keep full estate papers, Social Security numbers, royalty account numbers, signatures, and private correspondence out of a public worksheet or unverified upload. Use the [court's current informal-probate page](https://www.ndcourts.gov/legal-self-help/informal-probate) to locate the official guide, then ask a North Dakota lawyer whether the guide covers the particular situation. The court's guide says it does not answer every estate question and cautions readers who are unsure how to proceed to consult counsel.

## Five questions owners commonly ask

### Does Form 11 always apply when I inherit North Dakota mineral rights?

No. The court guide identifies Form 11 as a personal representative's deed of distribution for real property to heirs or devisees. It does not establish that a specific decedent owned the mineral interest, that it was probate property, that a particular person is entitled to it, or that this form fits the estate. Ask qualified North Dakota counsel to evaluate the actual ownership and estate documents. [North Dakota Courts guide, inventory and Form 11 sections](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf).

### Is Form 12 another name for a distribution to heirs?

No. In the guide, Form 12 is labeled as a personal representative's deed for a **sale** of real property, while Form 11 is labeled for **distribution** to heirs or devisees. That source distinction helps frame a records question; it does not advise an estate to sell or decide whether a prior transaction was valid. [North Dakota Courts guide, distribution and Form 12 sections](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf).

### Where does the guide say these deeds are recorded?

Both instruction sections point to the county recorder in the county where the real property is located and describe accompanying certified personal-representative letters. That does not tell you which county applies to an uncertain property description or whether the correct instrument appears in the index. Preserve the source reference and ask the recorder or qualified counsel about the particular record. [North Dakota Courts guide, Form 11 and Form 12 instructions](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf).

### Does a royalty check prove a probate deed transferred the interest?

No. A check or statement may point to an operator, payor, account, or property label, but it is not the court's estate record or a county-recorded chain. Keep payment and title questions separate. The [North Dakota royalty-record routing guide](/blog/north-dakota-inherited-royalty-questions-records-and-ombudsman/) explains where an operator-contact or payment question may go without treating a payment clue as ownership proof.

### What if the estate papers do not match either form label?

Do not force the facts into a self-help form. The court says its informal-administration packet does not address every estate question, and each form's instructions warn that deed-form assistance is outside the self-help center's scope. Bring the actual estate, ownership, and property references to a lawyer licensed in North Dakota. This article cannot decide if a trust, joint ownership, another estate path, or a prior conveyance controls. [North Dakota Courts guide, introduction and form cautions](https://www.ndcourts.gov/Media/Default/Legal%20Resources/Legal%20Self%20Help/Probate/Guidebook-for-Informal-Admin-of-Estate.pdf).
