# Private manuscript — Where Can I Find Ohio Mineral Deeds and Leases Before a Title Review?

**Unreviewed and unpublished.** Working article identity only; not a public MDX, admitted MRX1000 row, legal opinion, title search, or owner-count increment. Source screen: `ohio-recorder-mineral-records-private-screen.md`. Last source check: 2026-09-25 UTC.

If you are collecting Ohio mineral records for a title review, start with the recorder for the county where the property is located. Ohio law directs specified real-property instruments to that county and identifies deeds, certain mineral-interest notices and affidavits, and leases or lease memoranda among the recorder's official-record categories. Use the recorder's own search or copy instructions, save the **full instrument** and its recording locator, and keep a list of unresolved names, property descriptions, and missing periods. A search result helps assemble a review file; it does not establish that you own a particular mineral fraction or that a lease is in force. [Ohio Revised Code §§5301.25(A)](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5301.25), [317.08(A)](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-317.08).

## Find the right county office first

Use the property location and any legal description or document reference you already have to identify the relevant county. Ohio Revised Code [§5301.25(A)](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5301.25) supplies the county-of-premises recording rule for the instruments it covers. The [Ohio Recorders' Association](https://www.ohiorecorders.com/aws/ORA/pt/sp/home_page) offers a route to the state's 88 county recorder offices. Follow that county's current official instructions rather than assuming that one statewide portal contains every recorded deed or lease. A property-tax page, well location, payor statement, or buyer letter may supply a search clue, but it is not a substitute for the recorded instruments and qualified review of their effect.

Start a simple register with the county, names searched (including prior owners or a business name if known), instrument type, recording date, instrument/book-and-page number, property description, and a link or copy-request reference. Keep an image or certified copy if your reviewer asks for one. Record how each item was found, including spelling variants and the date you searched. Do not publish account numbers, signatures, personal addresses, or private royalty statements in a public question or search query.

## Which instrument categories should you look for?

Ohio Revised Code [§317.08(A)](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-317.08) lists record classes, not a decision that any particular document controls your minerals. Depending on your file, ask about:

| Potential record | Why it belongs in the collection file | What its presence alone cannot decide |
|---|---|---|
| Deed or other conveyance | Shows the language and parties in a recorded transfer that may bear on the identified property. | Whether later reservations, transfers, inheritance, or another instrument changed the interest. |
| Oil-and-gas lease, lease memorandum, supplement, modification, or amendment | Preserves the recorded text or reference a reviewer can compare with the owner's own lease documents. | Whether a lease currently covers the owner's tract or remains effective. |
| Relevant recorded notice, affidavit, or order | Flags a document that may need separate legal examination in the chain. | Whether a statutory process was satisfied or a mineral interest vested in anyone. |

The first two classes are identified in §317.08(A)(1) and (25); the notice/affidavit/order categories appear in §317.08(A)(2), (5), and (11). Do not file, rely on, or disregard a statutory notice or affidavit based on this checklist. Its application and deadlines require the actual documents and qualified Ohio legal advice.

## If the online search looks empty

Do not turn “no online result” into “no mineral deed” or “no lease.” Counties run their own record systems and have different digital coverage. As one limited example, the [Erie County Recorder FAQ](https://www.eriecounty.oh.gov/RecorderFAQs.aspx) says its online index and images begin in 1990 and older material is being added. It explains how to request a copy that is not available online. That date and procedure belong to **Erie County**, not every Ohio recorder.

For an **Erie County** example, its [current recorder FAQ](https://www.eriecounty.oh.gov/RecorderFAQs.aspx) links to the county-designated document search. It instructs users to enter a party name in the “Both Names” field as last name then first name, or to use another listed search category, and to view the result's document image. Record the exact input, result identifier, image availability, and search date; do not select a result as your tract's controlling document from a name match alone. This is an Erie workflow, not a statewide search recipe. The FAQ itself explains how to request a copy when the image is not online; it warns that recorder staff do not perform a full history or title search.

If you expected a document, use **your county's** recorder contact or copy-request path and provide the best known names, approximate date, property description, and any book/page or instrument number. Ask what historical index or in-office materials might cover the gap. Preserve the unanswered search as an open item rather than creating a conclusion. [Ashtabula County Recorder](https://www.ashtabulacounty.us/Recorder) likewise distinguishes record maintenance from title examination.

## Hand off a reviewable file, not a title conclusion

For a qualified Ohio mineral-title reviewer, send a dated list of the county and property identifiers, the full recorded documents and locators, any lease papers the family or payor supplied, and the questions you could not reconcile. Keep competing versions side by side. Ask the reviewer to determine what the instruments mean for the particular tract, minerals, depths, parties, and time period. A recorder index, an isolated deed, a lease memorandum, and a royalty payment each have different evidence roles. None alone computes an ownership fraction, decides a dormant-mineral issue, guarantees lease status, or establishes a fair sale price.

If you also have a written purchase offer, use the [offer-review pillar](/offer-review/) to keep the buyer's property-and-rights assumptions next to the recorded-source questions. If you are still researching, the [title, lease, and ownership pillar](/learning-center/title-lease-ownership/) is the better next step; no contact or booking is required merely to collect the public records.

### What if I only have an old owner's name?

Use the name as a starting search term in the **relevant county** and retain spelling, middle-name, entity-name, and date variants as hypotheses. A matching name is not proof that a recorded instrument concerns your tract or that the named person held the interest you are researching. Compare the full property description and document trail with a qualified reviewer.

### Does a recorded Ohio lease prove I am still being paid correctly?

No. A recorded lease or memorandum gives a document to examine, but it is not a private payor ledger. Current payment questions require the owner's statements and payor explanation, the actual lease and any amendments, and qualified review of the interest and applicable terms. The [royalties pillar](/learning-center/oil-and-gas-royalties/) covers the statement-reading path without treating the recorder index as a payment audit.

### Can a county recorder tell me who owns the minerals?

The office maintains and makes recorded documents accessible. It does not issue a tract-specific mineral-title opinion. [Ashtabula County](https://www.ashtabulacounty.us/Recorder) explicitly says its recorder is not authorized to perform title examinations, and [Erie County](https://www.eriecounty.oh.gov/RecorderFAQs.aspx) says its office does not conduct complete history/title searches. Ask the recorder for search and copy instructions; ask a qualified professional to analyze disputed ownership or legal effect.
