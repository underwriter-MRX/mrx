**No.** A Texas Railroad Commission (RRC) drilling permit records regulatory permission and related filing details for a proposed well. It does not establish that your private mineral lease is valid, covers the relevant tract or depth, includes the proposed wellbore, or entitles you to royalties. The RRC expressly says it has no jurisdiction over the validity of private mineral leases. Its public permit result uses “Lease” as a regulatory label for lease, pooled, or unitized acreage, not as a title opinion about your contract. [RRC Permian Basin FAQs](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/major-formation-faqs/permian-basin/) · [RRC permit-query results help](https://webapps2.rrc.texas.gov/EWA/help/DPQuery_results_howto.htm)

This is a public-record comparison, not an interpretation of any owner's lease, title, pooling arrangement, acreage, wellbore, royalties, or legal rights. A qualified Texas land or title professional should review disputed private rights. Do not send a full unredacted lease or personal records through an untrusted channel. MRX may have an economic interest in a later transaction and discloses that relationship before an agreement.

That distinction does not mean an operator can drill without private rights. The [RRC FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/major-formation-faqs/permian-basin/) says an operator must have a legal mineral lease for extraction, but that requirement is different from the agency determining whether **your specific** lease covers **this specific** well. Treat a permit as a record to compare with private documents, not as proof of owner-specific coverage.

## What a permit can tell you

The RRC's [drilling-permit result guide](https://webapps2.rrc.texas.gov/EWA/help/DPQuery_results_howto.htm) identifies fields such as API number, county of the surface hole, regulatory lease name, well number, permitted operator, status and status date, filing purpose, and planned wellbore profile. A result can link to the current W-1 application, current permit, attachments listed on the W-1 screen, and a plat. Record the exact identifier and date from the document you actually open; the result row and its attachments are not interchangeable.

The guide explains that the displayed “Lease” name describes acreage for RRC regulatory purposes and may change at completion. The displayed operator is the applicant or permit holder for Commission purposes. Neither label substitutes for a recorded lease, a chain of title, or professional interpretation of a private agreement.

If you need a reproducible search trail, use the separate [Texas RRC drilling-permit query retrieval provenance worksheet](/blog/texas-rrc-drilling-permit-query-retrieval-provenance-worksheet/). That worksheet records how a query was run; this article explains why a result cannot answer a private-lease question by itself.

## Compare the two record sets without merging them

Use a short exception list rather than deciding from a matching name. Keep these comparisons separate:

- Record the W-1, permit number, API, and status date. Locate the owner's recorded deed or assignment and lease separately. Ask whether they describe the same interest and time period.
- Preserve the regulatory lease name, operator, and filed plat. Then inspect the private lease parties, legal description, and any amendments. Ask whether similar names are merely labels or the instruments connect the tract.
- Preserve the planned wellbore profile and locations shown in the filing. Locate any relevant pooling or unit documents and tract or depth language. Ask which instruments, if any, address this tract and interval.

Write `not matched`, `document missing`, or `professional review needed` for a gap. Do not write `covered` or `not covered` from a public search alone. The RRC's [well-records guide](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/obtaining-commission-records/oil-and-gas-well-records/) identifies W-1 applications and location plats as agency well records and says the agency lacks authority over lease, royalty, and property-rights matters. For the private-record side, start with the county of the property and the recorded instruments; the [Texas GLO mineral-rights FAQ](https://www.glo.texas.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Minerals%20FAQ_updated%202023.pdf) explains why deed and lease examination matters. A qualified professional may need additional records not apparent from these public indexes.

## A surface-location mismatch is not the answer

A horizontal well can be drilled from a surface site outside the lease designated on its permit. The RRC describes this possibility in its [Permian Basin FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/major-formation-faqs/permian-basin/). A surface pin outside your tract therefore neither proves exclusion nor inclusion. The well path, filed plat, relevant private instruments, and any unit documents need their own review. Do not infer a royalty share, trespass, or a remedy from a map pin.

An application is not the same event as an issued permit, a drilled well, a completed well, or reported production. The [RRC result guide](https://webapps2.rrc.texas.gov/EWA/help/DPQuery_results_howto.htm) explains status and approval dates. Preserve the actual status shown when checked rather than assuming activity progressed.

## The useful next step

If the RRC record and your private documents appear to concern the same place, assemble the exact W-1 or permit and plat reference, the recorded deed or assignment, your lease and amendments, and any identified pooling or unit papers. Note what you could not match. A Texas land or title professional can assess that defined question; this public article cannot.
