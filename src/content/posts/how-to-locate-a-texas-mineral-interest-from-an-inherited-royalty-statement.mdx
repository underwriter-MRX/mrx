> **Location clues, not proof of ownership.** This guide helps an heir organize identifiers from an old Texas royalty statement and identify a possible county-record search path. It does not determine the property, heirship, title, payment entitlement, royalty decimal, value, or whether to sell. MRX may be a disclosed buyer in a later transaction; its educational review is directional and is not a certified appraisal or legal, tax, title, accounting, engineering, investment, or financial advice.

If you inherited a Texas royalty statement but do not know **which county or tract it concerns**, start with the payor and property clues actually printed on it. Using a contact route you verify independently, ask the payor to explain how its internal reference connects to a lease, well, Railroad Commission identifier, and county. Use the resulting public-record clues to choose a county deed and probate research path, but leave ownership unresolved until the record chain is reviewed.

That is a **location workflow**; it does not prove you own an interest. The [Railroad Commission of Texas (RRC) royalties FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) warns that the identifier on a payment stub may not be the RRC identification number needed for production research.

## First, preserve the statement without exposing it

Save a complete copy of the inherited check stub or royalty statement and note its statement date, the person or estate named, and how you obtained it. On a private worksheet, transcribe only fields the document actually shows. Texas's [RRC royalty-statement guidance](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) identifies property name or number and county/state among information an owner may receive, but statement layouts vary and an old copy may omit a field.

| Clue to look for, if printed | What to record privately | What it does **not** settle |
|---|---|---|
| Payor name and contact information | Exact payor label, statement date, and verified contact route | Whether the payor is the current operator or the heir's title is clear |
| Prior owner's name or owner/account number | The printed name and a controlled reference to the number | Whether that number is an RRC lease or well ID |
| Property, lease, unit, or well label | Exact spelling and any printed property reference | Whether a similarly named public record is the same tract |
| County or state, if shown | The printed jurisdiction and source line | Whether one field or lease lies wholly in that county |
| Production or sales month | The period printed on the statement | Whether the statement date is the production month |

Keep the full account number, mailing address, tax identifier, bank information, check image, and estate documents out of public searches, screenshots, and AI chats. If you need to show a question to someone, redact these details and share sensitive records only through a verified private channel. This is a privacy practice, not a claim that a redacted image establishes the property.

## Ask the verified payor for the missing crosswalk

Find the payor's current owner-relations contact from its own official website or an independently confirmed prior communication. Do not reply to a new address merely because it appears on an unsolicited offer. With the relevant authorization or estate representative, ask the payor which property its statement reference denotes, whether its number differs from the RRC identifier, and which lease, well, county or counties, and state it has recorded. Ask for the source and date of each answer and whether multiple properties share the account.

The [RRC royalties FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) explains that a Texas royalty owner may request the RRC identification number for a lease, property, or well from the payor under Texas Natural Resources Code § 91.504. The legal request process and who is authorized to make it depend on the facts; an heir who has not completed the estate or payor transfer should ask the payor or qualified adviser what proof of authority is needed. Do not promise that the payor can provide a perfect historical crosswalk.

If the payor cannot confirm what an old number meant, record that answer as **unresolved**. Do not silently substitute a nearby well, similar lease name, buyer letter, or county-tax hit.

## Use RRC production records as context, not title

Once you have a plausible RRC identifier or lease name, the [RRC Production Data Query FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/production-data-query-system-faqs/) explains the system's lease, field, operator, district, and county search paths. Its guidance also says a field or lease can be in **more than one county**. A matching name or number may help locate candidate records; it does not connect the statement to a particular private mineral interest.

Use the [official RRC Research Queries directory](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/resource-center/research/research-queries/) and follow the site's own access rules. Its online-query notice warns against automated retrieval that burdens the system; this guide does not instruct readers or software to scrape it. Record the query route, exact criteria, access date, displayed result, and any ambiguity. For a detailed provenance template after an identifier is known, use the separate [RRC retrieval worksheet](/blog/texas-rrc-production-data-query-retrieval-provenance-worksheet/).

Operator-reported production may lag or be revised; the [RRC PDQ FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/production-data-query-system-faqs/) describes those timing limits. Separately, treat RRC production data as contextual information, not as a private payor ledger, property match, or legal title determination.

## Then choose the correct county record sources

With a county *candidate*, look for recorded deeds, reservations, assignments, and other conveyances in the county where the land lies. The [Texas State Library's property-research guide](https://www.tsl.texas.gov/ref/propertyresearch) says divisions and transfers of property ownership are filed in those counties. Research estate or probate papers as a **separate source class**; the [Texas State Library county-record guide](https://www.tsl.texas.gov/arc/local/index) explains that county records may include deeds, probate files, and wills, with availability and older record formats varying by county.

Do not treat a county search result, a single deed, a probate index entry, or an RRC match as proof of the heir's current title. The known-county [Texas ownership guide](/blog/how-to-find-out-if-you-own-mineral-rights-in-texas/) starts at the recorded instrument chain and explains why gaps, reservations, and later transfers may require a qualified title professional or Texas attorney.

## Fictional example: preserve the unresolved link

This example is fictional. It illustrates a research record, not a real statement, payor, property, county, or title conclusion.

| Register field | Fictional entry |
|---|---|
| Starting artifact | Old statement retained privately; prior-owner name and payment period visible |
| Printed property reference | `[private payor reference]`; no county or legal description shown |
| Payor question | “Which lease or well and RRC identifier did this reference denote for the stated period, and which county or counties were in your records?” |
| Public record candidate | None until a verified payor answer gives a source-linked identifier |
| Status | **Unresolved**: no county, tract, heirship, or title conclusion |
| Next step | Verify authority and payor contact; request the crosswalk; preserve the dated reply |

A later RRC hit with a similar lease name would be another clue, **not** permission to fill the county cell without confirming the match. If the evidence points to two counties, keep both candidates and ask for a narrower source rather than choosing one.

## Stop when the linkage is uncertain

Stop self-service matching when the statement cannot be tied to a verified payor record; when an identifier yields several properties or counties; when a prior owner's name differs from the recorded chain; or when estate, trust, or deed records conflict. Ask the payor to clarify its own account record. Ask the appropriate county office how to retrieve the record you have identified, and use a qualified land or Texas legal professional for owner-specific title, probate, or conveyance analysis.
