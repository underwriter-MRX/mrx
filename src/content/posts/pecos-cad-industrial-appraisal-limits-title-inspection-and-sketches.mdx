> **Industrial-plan source guide, not proof of a property fact.** This article records the assumptions and limiting conditions on physical page 49 of Pecos County Appraisal District's 2025-2026 Reappraisal Plan. It does not establish title, a legal description, the absence of liens, an inspection, a boundary, an engineering conclusion, a hidden condition, a mineral-interest value, a tax or protest position, or a transaction result. MRX may have an economic interest in a later mineral transaction. When that applies, the buyer relationship is disclosed in writing before an agreement is signed.

The direct answer is: **Pecos CAD's industrial appraisal section tells readers not to treat the appraisal record as proof of title, inspection, surveying, engineering, or hidden conditions**. The plan assumes title and legal-description facts, says every property is not necessarily inspected each year, and calls sketches visual aids rather than surveys or engineering reports unless otherwise specified.

That is also an industrial-property section. The plan defines its scope as processing facilities and related personal property. It is not the oil-and-gas reserve section and should not be applied to a mineral interest.

## Direct answer: what the industrial record does not prove

| Plan statement on physical page 49                 | Safe reading                                                                     | Do not infer                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Title and legal description are assumed            | The appraisal proceeds from stated assumptions.                                  | Proven title, a title opinion, or a confirmed legal description.                  |
| Liens and encumbrances are disregarded             | The appraisal treats the property as free and clear for its stated purpose.      | That no lien, mortgage, or encumbrance exists.                                    |
| Every property is not necessarily inspected yearly | Inspection frequency cannot be established from the appraisal record alone.      | That a named property was or was not inspected in a named year.                   |
| Sketches are visual aids                           | A sketch can help a reader understand the appraisal document.                    | A survey, boundary determination, dimension certification, or engineering report. |
| Appraisals are for ad valorem tax purposes         | The record belongs to the local taxable-property appraisal process.              | A private mineral value, transaction opinion, or investment conclusion.           |
| Hidden conditions cannot be personally observed    | The plan limits representations about subsurface and concealed structural facts. | A clean condition finding or an engineering conclusion about hidden items.        |

## “Title is assumed” is not a title finding

Item 1 says title to the property is assumed to be good and marketable and the legal description correct. The verb matters: **assumed** is not **verified**.

Item 2 adds that no responsibility for legal matters is assumed. It says liens, mortgages, and other encumbrances are disregarded and the property is appraised as though free and clear. That establishes an appraisal premise, not the real-world absence of a recorded or unrecorded burden.

Keep three records separate:

1. the appraisal record and its stated assumptions;
2. the current deed, conveyance, reservation, assignment, probate, or other ownership chain; and
3. any title examination or legal opinion prepared for the actual question.

This article does not examine any of those property-specific records or decide which one controls.

## “Not necessarily inspected every year” leaves the date unresolved

Item 4 says the appraisers do not necessarily inspect every property every year. It does not say that no properties are inspected, that one named property was skipped, or that an old observation controls a current result.

If inspection timing matters, record the exact property, the exact appraisal year, the official source of the claimed date, and what the source says was inspected. Stop if the date or scope cannot be tied to the named property. A general plan statement cannot fill that gap.

The [Texas Comptroller's Property Tax System Basics page](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/basics.php) explains that appraisal districts appraise local property values and directs readers to the local district for local appraisal-process details. That general responsibility does not prove a Pecos inspection date.

## A sketch can orient a reader without establishing a boundary

Item 5 says appraisal-document sketches are intended as visual aids and should not be construed as surveys or engineering reports unless otherwise specified. Preserve both halves of the statement:

- the default role is **visual aid**; and
- a different role requires the document to **specify** it.

Do not use an ordinary appraisal sketch to certify acreage, establish a boundary, locate an easement, prove a facility dimension, or make a structural conclusion. Those questions require the relevant current official record and, when needed, an appropriately qualified professional.

## Hidden conditions remain outside ordinary observation

Item 8 says appraisers inspect improvements as far as possible by observation, then limits what can be personally observed beneath the soil or within hidden structural components. It says no representations are made about those matters unless an individual appraisal specifically considers them.

That is a source limitation, not a warning that a defect exists. It supports neither a clean-condition conclusion nor an adverse-condition conclusion. Record the hidden-condition question as unresolved until property-specific evidence addresses it.

## Ad valorem purpose is not a private mineral valuation

Item 7 says the appraisals were prepared exclusively for ad valorem tax purposes. The [Texas Comptroller's Valuing Property page](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/valuing-property.php) explains the statewide taxable-property context, including January 1 valuation timing, mass appraisal, and common cost, income, and sales-comparison approaches.

That context does not turn an industrial appraisal record into a private mineral acquisition valuation. A private mineral question may require separate ownership, lease, production, engineering, price, timing, risk, market, buyer, and transaction evidence. None is supplied by the industrial assumptions page.

## A four-question separation worksheet

Before relying on an industrial appraisal record for a different question, write down:

| Verification question | Evidence needed                                                 | Stop condition                                                         |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What property type?   | The section, account category, and property actually covered.   | Stop if industrial and oil-and-gas scopes are being mixed.             |
| What fact is assumed? | The exact statement and whether it is an assumption or finding. | Stop if an assumption is being presented as verified fact.             |
| What work occurred?   | Property-specific date, scope, method, and official record.     | Stop if inspection, survey, or engineering work cannot be shown.       |
| What purpose applies? | The stated appraisal purpose and the question being asked.      | Stop if an ad valorem record is being used as a private value opinion. |

This worksheet is an evidence-separation aid. It does not establish title, direct an appraiser, interpret a survey, provide an engineering opinion, calculate tax, or determine a mineral value.
