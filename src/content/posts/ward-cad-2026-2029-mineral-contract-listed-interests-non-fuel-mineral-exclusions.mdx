The direct answer is: **Ward CAD's professional appraisal services contract defines “Mineral Property” as a contract category covering producing fuel and hard-mineral properties and interests in them.** It lists working interests, oil payments, overriding royalties, royalty interests, and other related interests. It expressly excludes sand, gravel, caliche, and other non-fuel minerals.

The contract defines **Personal Property** separately for property used or employed in exploration and production. It covers tax years 2026 through 2029, names January 1 as the annual information date, and places the work in an ad valorem appraisal context. Those statements describe the contract. They do not decide what a particular person owns, whether a specific asset is taxable, or what private mineral rights are worth.

## What the contract puts inside Mineral Property

Packet page 26 describes a producing-property category and then gives examples of interests in that property. The wording uses both “including” and a final reference to other related interests, so the listed items should not be recast as an exhaustive title taxonomy.

The page supports the following bounded reading:

- **Producing oil, gas, coal, other hydrocarbons, or hard minerals:** this is the contract's subject-matter boundary. It does not prove that a specific tract is producing or owned by a reader.
- **Working interests:** this is one expressly listed interest type. It does not prove a working-interest percentage, burdens, title, or value.
- **Oil payments:** this is one expressly listed interest type. It does not prove a payment amount, duration, validity, or tax treatment.
- **Overriding royalties:** this is one expressly listed interest type. It does not prove a chain of title, decimal interest, production, or value.
- **Royalty interests:** this is one expressly listed interest type. It does not prove ownership, lease terms, payment status, or market value.
- **Other related interests:** the list is not written as closed. That does not create unlimited coverage or a legal conclusion about an unlisted interest.

This is a definition in one appraisal-services contract. It is useful for understanding the scope assigned to the appraisal firm, but it is not a substitute for a mineral deed, lease, division order, title opinion, tax account, production record, or professional appraisal.

## What the contract expressly excludes

The same paragraph excludes four groups from its Mineral Property definition:

- sand;
- gravel;
- caliche; and
- all other non-fuel minerals.

That is a contract-scope statement. It does not settle every legal, tax, ownership, land-use, or commercial question involving those materials. It also does not mean that an excluded material is irrelevant to every other appraisal or property category. The safe statement is narrower: the Ward contract does not place those non-fuel materials inside its defined Mineral Property class.

The distinction matters because “mineral” can be used differently in deeds, statutes, appraisal systems, engineering reports, and ordinary conversation. A researcher should preserve the source's defined term instead of treating one document's category as a universal definition.

## Mineral Property and Personal Property are separate classes

Packet page 26 defines Personal Property separately. Its examples involve property used or employed in exploration and production, such as rigs, service-company property, supply-store property, trucking and dirt-contractor property, tank farms, and field compressors. The clause also allows other mutually agreed personal property to be included in the contract.

The document therefore uses two different lanes:

1. Mineral Property describes producing mineral properties and interests in them, subject to the stated exclusions.
2. Personal Property describes equipment, business property, and related property used in exploration and production.

The distinction is documentary and does not establish facts about a particular asset. A pump, compressor, royalty interest, or payment right still needs its own authoritative records and applicable professional review before anyone assigns ownership, classification, tax, or value consequences.

## The 2026-2029 term and January 1 date

Contract page 1 identifies tax years **2026, 2027, 2028, and 2029**. Contract page 2 states that the appraisal firm will gather reasonably needed and available information as of **January 1** of each covered year for the described ad valorem appraisal work.

The current [Texas Tax Code Section 23.01](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?artSec=23.01&chapter=TX.23&code=TX&tab=1) separately states the general January 1 market-value date for taxable property, subject to the chapter's exceptions. It also addresses generally accepted appraisal methods and the professional-standards requirement when an appraisal district uses mass-appraisal standards.

Those are public-appraisal timing and method statements. January 1 is not automatically:

- the date an owner acquired an interest;
- the effective date of a deed or lease;
- a production month;
- an offer date;
- a private valuation date; or
- the date a contract document was published online.
