A clawback clause in a Texas mineral rights sale is a provision that may let a buyer seek a refund, reduction, offset, holdback, or other remedy after closing if the written agreement says specific conditions are met. Owners often consider the clause carefully because it can change what the headline number actually means under the agreement.

This post is general information, not legal advice. Verify any specific situation with a Texas-licensed attorney.

For statutory context, [Texas Property Code Chapter 5](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/pdf/PR.5.pdf) addresses real-property conveyances and includes disclosure rules for certain mailed offers to purchase mineral or royalty interests. The code does not replace document-specific legal review of a proposed agreement.

## What the clause does

A clawback clause may give the buyer a right to demand a refund, a price reduction, an offset, a re-conveyance, or another remedy if the agreement's stated trigger occurs. Possible triggers can include:

- A title issue the seller did not disclose (a prior lien, an outstanding royalty interest, an undisclosed heir).
- A material misrepresentation in the seller's representations and warranties.
- A production-volume or revenue-volume claim that turns out to be inaccurate.
- A post-close obligation the seller agreed to (recordkeeping, sign-off on operator changes) that the seller did not perform.

The clause may also define a response deadline, a remedy period, a cap, exclusions, notice requirements, or a specific method for calculating the requested amount. Those details come from the actual agreement, not from a universal market rule.

<CalloutBox variant="info" title="The trade-off">
  A clawback clause can shift some post-closing risk to the seller if the written trigger, remedy,
  and timing language allows it. The practical trade-off depends on the agreement language, the
  property records, the amount at issue, and the seller's professional advice.
</CalloutBox>

## Why buyers use it

Some mineral-rights agreements include clawback language to allocate risk for title, representation, production, revenue, or post-closing issues. The reason and effect depend on the specific transaction documents and the facts behind them.

The clause is not evaluated in isolation. The questions are what the agreement says, what facts support the trigger, what process applies before money or rights change hands, and which professional should review the language.

## What to look for

When reading a clawback clause, owners often consider a few specific points. None of these is advice; they are questions worth asking a Texas-licensed attorney.

- **Time period.** How long after closing can the buyer invoke the clause, and what date or event starts and ends that period?
- **Cap.** Does the clause state a maximum amount, and how is that amount calculated?
- **Evidence and process.** What facts must be shown before the remedy applies, who must provide them, and what written process controls disputes?
- **Exclusions.** What is excluded from the clause, and does the exclusion language match the seller's situation?
- **Notice and cure.** Does the buyer have to give the seller notice and a chance to respond or cure before invoking the clause?
- **Offset rights.** Can the seller offset against other interests, or is the clawback a hard refund demand?

## A few related terms

Owners often encounter a few related terms at the same time. None is advice; they are labels to ask about.

- **Representations and warranties.** The seller's statements about the property, the title, the production, and the absence of liens. Read whether the clawback clause is tied to those statements.
- **Indemnification.** A separate obligation that may survive closing and may require reimbursement if the written conditions are met.
- **Escrow holdback.** A portion of the purchase price that may be held under the written escrow terms, if the agreement uses that structure.
- **Bring-down certificate.** A document that may ask the seller to confirm representations at closing or another stated date, if the agreement requires it.

## When to involve a Texas-licensed attorney

If any of the following apply, an owner may want to ask a Texas-licensed attorney to review the agreement before signing or closing:

- The clause could reduce, recover, offset, or delay a material part of the stated consideration.
- The time period, cap, exclusions, notice process, or dispute process is missing or unclear.
- The evidence standard or response process is hard to understand from the agreement language.
- The seller holds a partial mineral interest, an interest with multiple heirs, or an interest with an unclear depth of rights.

A Texas-licensed attorney can read the clause in the context of the rest of the agreement and tell the owner what the trade-off actually means.

## A short summary

A clawback clause can shift post-closing risk if the written agreement allows a refund, reduction, offset, holdback, re-conveyance, or other remedy after a stated trigger. Read the time period, cap, evidence standard, exclusions, notice-and-cure rights, offset rights, and dispute process together, and consider attorney review before signing or closing when the language is material or unclear.
