<h2 id="answer-first">Direct answer for mineral owners</h2>
The MRX platform uses AI to make the beginning of a mineral-rights review more structured. It can organize an owner's question, identify missing facts, connect the question to cited public context, and prepare a clearer handoff for a human underwriter. The difference is the workflow and its visible boundaries, not a claim that an algorithm can settle title, predict production, or decide what an owner should do.

## What the AI-first step does

Mineral-rights questions often arrive as a mixture of documents, family history, check-stub terms, offer language, and uncertainty. The AI-first step separates that mixture into practical categories:

- location and identity details, such as state, county, operator, lease, or well;
- ownership context, including inherited, purchased, leased, or royalty-only interests;
- records already available, such as deeds, leases, division orders, royalty statements, or offers;
- the owner's immediate question, such as a payment change, offer comparison, or document gap; and
- facts that remain unknown and should not be guessed.

That organization can make a later conversation more useful because the underwriter can see what is known, what is owner-supplied, what comes from a public source, and what still requires verification.

## What remains a human or professional question

AI output does not establish ownership, interpret a deed for a specific legal outcome, give tax treatment, certify value, or guarantee a sale result. A public record may show reported production or an operator name, but it may not match the interest described in an owner's documents. A royalty check may show a decimal, but it does not by itself prove the complete chain of title.

When the question turns on a deed, estate, tax return, contract remedy, or certified valuation, the appropriate qualified professional should review the actual facts. The platform's job is to make those boundaries easier to see.

## How the review workflow fits together

### 1. Start with the owner's question

The owner can describe the issue without uploading a private file. A useful first message might name the county and operator, explain that checks changed, or ask what an offer clause means in plain language.

### 2. Organize the file

The workflow identifies which facts are confirmed, which are assumptions, and which documents may answer the next question. Owners should avoid uploading Social Security numbers, bank details, signatures, or unrelated personal information.

### 3. Add public context

The [Railroad Commission's research tools](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/resource-center/research/research-queries/) can provide reported production, well, lease, operator, permit, and related context. Public data is useful evidence, but it is not a title opinion or a stand-alone pricing answer.

### 4. Apply a stated methodology

MRX's [published methodology](/methodology/) describes a directional discounted-cash-flow review using production history, decline context, royalty terms, commodity assumptions, risk, title confidence, and offer language when available. The output is a range with assumptions stated, not a regulated or certified appraisal.

### 5. Keep the owner in control

The owner chooses whether to share more information, book a review, compare an offer, sell, keep the interest, or take more time. MRX provides educational information and resources only and does not buy mineral rights.

## Privacy and source transparency

The [MRX privacy policy](/privacy-policy/) describes AI-assisted processing, private document handling, service providers, retention, and owner choices. The practical rule is simple: use only the information needed for the question, keep sensitive identifiers out of uploads, and distinguish owner-provided records from public-source context.

## Questions to ask any AI-assisted mineral-rights service

- Does the service identify which statements are facts, assumptions, or unresolved questions?
- Does it cite the public source behind a production or regulatory statement?
- Does it clearly say what the AI cannot determine?
- Does it explain how documents and personal information are handled?
- Does a human review the file before a directional range or transaction discussion?
- Is the owner free to stop without selling?

Those questions are more useful than a broad claim that one technology is automatically more accurate than another.
